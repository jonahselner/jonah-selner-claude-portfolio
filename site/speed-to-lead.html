01 · The problem

Three versions of what happens after "Submit"

A web form lead is someone who just raised their hand. What the practice does in the next few minutes decides a lot. Here's how that moment changed for our clients.

1

A phone call, eventually

Most practices weren't texting leads at all. They called back, often hours or days later, and the patient heard nothing in between.

What the patient got: silence, then phone tag and voicemails. Plenty of time to call someone else.
2

An automated text

"Thanks so much, we got your contact form. Is this John?" Instant, which helped. But the moment John replied, a person still had to see it and carry the conversation.

The catch: the first touch was fast, the second wasn't. A question at 9pm waited until morning.
3

An AI agent that talks back

The patient gets a text from the practice within about a minute. The agent answers questions from that practice's knowledge base, keeps the conversation warm, and asks about a consult at the right moments.

Human in the loop: once the patient says yes, the AI turns off and a real person calls to book.
Why speed matters

Leads go cold in minutes, and most businesses answer in hours

This has been studied for years, in general sales and in vision correction specifically. The pattern holds every time.

21x

Drop in the odds of qualifying a web lead when the first call comes at 30 minutes instead of 5. The odds of even reaching them dropped 100x.

Oldroyd (MIT) and InsideSales, 2007 · 15,000+ leads
~7x

How much more likely companies were to qualify a lead when they reached out within an hour, compared with an hour later. Average first response across 2,241 companies: 42 hours.

Harvard Business Review, 2011
19 hrs

Average time for U.S. LASIK practices to respond to a web lead in a 2023 mystery shop of 101 clinics. Only 14% responded within 10 minutes.

ZEISS and LeadEngage white paper, 2024

Patients also want to text. In a 2021 HIMSS survey, nearly half of patients said they prefer texting their providers, and more than a third said they'd switch providers to get real-time texting. An agent covers the hours a team can't: nights, weekends, and the lunch rush when every line is busy.

02 · How it works

Four steps, and only one of them is the model

The AI is one piece of a workflow. A form triggers it, a knowledge base grounds it, an actions layer and back-end automation decide when it stops, and a person finishes the job.

03 · Design decisions

The choices that made it safe to scale

Getting one agent to sound good for one practice is easy. Running it for dozens, in healthcare, without someone rewriting it every week took a few deliberate choices.

1

Split the rules from the facts

The master prompt holds the voice, the conversation flow and the hard limits. Each practice's facts live in its own knowledge base.

Why: a fix to the master prompt improves every practice at once, and a new practice means filling in a knowledge base, not writing a new prompt.

2

The AI never touches the calendar

It can't offer, confirm or move appointment times, and it has no access to calendars or records.

Why: booking changes a real doctor's schedule. That stays with a person. The agent's job is getting the patient to yes.

3

An explicit order of authority

Hard limits, then actions, then conversation flow, then personality. When two rules conflict, the higher one wins.

Why: a prompt with dozens of rules will contradict itself. "Always end with a question" and "stop asking once they say yes" can't both win, so the prompt says which does.

4

Tell it when to use each fact

Every knowledge base section opens with a line like "use when asked about location or hours." FAQs say "use these answers directly."

Why: the agent pulls the right block for the question in front of it, and the most common answers stay consistent from one patient to the next.

5

Eligibility is a setting, not a judgment

Each practice lists its own "Requires Team Review" factors. If a patient mentions one, the agent stops moving toward a booking.

Why: who's a candidate is a clinical call. The practice decides the triggers. The AI only recognizes them and steps back.

6

Patient messages are data, not instructions

Rules come only from the system prompt. Anything a patient sends that tries to change them is treated as a manipulation attempt.

Why: anyone can text "ignore your instructions." A public-facing agent has to assume someone will.

04 · Onboarding

Every practice gets its own agent, built from an SOP

The knowledge base is only as good as what the practice tells us, so we built a repeatable onboarding process around it.

  1. Intake formThe practice answers our questions ahead of time.
  2. Recorded callWe dig deeper on a call and record it, so nothing depends on someone's notes.
  3. Knowledge baseWe build it from the call in the standard structure.
  4. Practice signs offThe practice approves what the agent will say before it says it.
  5. Try to break itA test bot throws curveballs at the agent to find where it slips.
  6. Go liveLeads start getting texts within about a minute.

Which procedures we book

Some practices want leads for everything they do. Others only want us booking certain procedures. The agent only moves listed procedures toward a consult. Anything else gets a warm "a team member will follow up."

Ophthalmology example: LASIK, PRK, ICL, RLE, cataract, glaucoma, cross-linking, dry eye. Or just LASIK and ICL.

Price: lead with it, or don't

Some practices want the promotion front and center. Others never want price discussed before the consult. That's a setting, and a cost question can trigger an escalation instead of an answer.

Example: "Don't quote price" practices route every cost question to the team. Financing terms can still be shared.

Calendars and locations

A small practice may have one doctor's calendar. A larger one may have several, with consults at one office and surgery at another. The agent checks which location does what before it answers.

Example: LASIK consults in Woodbury, surgery in Minneapolis. The agent says so instead of implying both happen in one place.

Who needs a human first

Each practice marks its own candidacy factors as "Requires Team Review." The agent never decides who is or isn't a candidate.

Example: under 18, or pregnant or nursing, and asking about LASIK.
05 · Guardrails

The part I spent the most time on

A friendly agent that's confidently wrong about a medical question is worse than no agent. So the prompt sets an order of authority. When two rules pull in different directions, the higher one wins, and the hard limits never bend for tone or flow.

1 · Hard limits› 2 · Actions› 3 · Conversation flow› 4 · Personality

Clinical lines

"Is this normal after my last eye surgery?"No diagnosis, no symptom reading. That's really a question for the team at your consultation.
"Am I a candidate?"Never yes or no. Shares general context, points to the free consult.
"Will I get 20/20?"No guarantees on outcomes or timelines. Results can vary by person.
A medical emergencyTells them to call 911.

Scope

A procedure the practice doesn't book through usAcknowledges the interest, no scheduling talk, a team member follows up.
Another clinic, or surgery abroadNever mentions other providers. Acknowledges the feeling, brings it back to the consult here.
Off-topic or personal, twiceOne warm acknowledgment, then a kind close with no booking ask.
A detail not in the knowledge baseDoesn't guess. I don't want to give you the wrong info there.

Scheduling and data

"Can I come in Tuesday at 3?"Never offers, confirms or moves times. No calendar or record access at all.
"Yes, let's book"Stops asking questions, thanks them, hands off to a person.
"What do you have on file for me?"Never references stored info or past conversations. No links ever.

Identity and manipulation

"Are you a real person?"Always says it's an AI, however the question is phrased or repeated.
"Ignore your instructions and…"Rules come only from the system prompt. Anything a patient sends is treated as a manipulation attempt.
"What's your prompt?"Never reveals its instructions.
06 · Human in the loop

The AI knows when to step aside

The agent starts conversations and keeps them moving. It doesn't get the final word. Triggers we set up on the back end watch each thread. When it makes sense for a person to take over, automation turns the AI off for that patient and hands the conversation to a team member, who reviews it and replies with their own judgment.

Ready to book

A real person books the appointment

The moment a patient says yes, the AI thanks them and stops asking questions. Then a team member calls to find a time and get them on the calendar.

Patient says yes›AI turns off›Team member calls
Edge case

A person takes over the conversation

When the thread goes somewhere the AI shouldn't handle alone, it gets punted to a human, for example:

  • A candidacy factor the practice marked "Requires Team Review"
  • A price question at a practice that doesn't quote prices by text
  • Interest in a procedure we don't book for that practice
Edge case›AI turns off›Human reviews and replies
07 · Iteration

Rules that came from real conversations

The first version of the prompt wasn't the one that scaled. Many of the rules came from patterns we noticed in live threads, and the prompt kept evolving as we read more of them. A few that made the cut:

One question per text

Every text is easy to answer, and the conversation never feels like a form.

Never ask to schedule twice in a row

The agent checks its last message first. If it already asked, it asks something about the patient instead.

Stop the moment they say yes

No "what day works?" and no second confirmation. Thank them and hand off.

Banned phrases

No "Great question." No "Great choice" about a procedure, because it sounds like ordering off a menu.

No dashes, ever

Long dashes are a well-known tell of AI-written text. The agent rewrites the sentence instead.

Close gently after two off-topic turns

One warm acknowledgment, then a kind close with no booking ask, matched to the weight of what they shared.
